# Pattern maps and construction instructions

The chore coat is the first annotated design page. Its seven piece numbers, matching seam codes, pocket marks, and construction steps provide a reviewable example. Its current pilot derives labels from draft geometry for each size and option set.

## Design-local pilot records

The skirt coat and pantry jacket have committed `construction.yaml` records (`44a22cb`). Their top-level fields are `design`, `shellDrape`, `pieces`, `cutPieces`, `seams`, `placements`, `finishing`, `stitches`, and `steps`. `pieces` maps drape roles to drafted parts; `cutPieces` records stable printed numbers and source IDs. The seam entries reference existing drape seam IDs and proposed stitch/finish/order data, and the steps reference those seams and pieces. These files are inputs for the shared pipeline, not a connected renderer yet. Pantry-jacket buttonholes have `draftMarksPresent: false`; this must remain a validation gap until marks are drafted. The chore-coat pilot now carries its map into the 3D input; the other designs' 3D export still drops pocket paths and stitch metadata.

## Verified generation paths (2026-11-20)

- The design-page PNG is generated by `designs/tools/preview.mjs`: `render.mjs` drafts with the FreeSewing theme and calls `pattern.render()`, then Playwright screenshots the SVG at 1000 px. `site/scripts/generate.mjs` copies that PNG to `site/static/img/previews/`. The former fixed-coordinate React overlay was absent from that PNG and from downloads; it has since been removed.
- The site's download buttons and `designs/tools/export.mjs` both call `designs/lib/export/index.mjs::exportFiles`. That drafts a separate pattern, converts it to plain millimetre geometry with `pieces()`, then independently calls `toLayeredSvg()` and `toProjectorPdf()`. Both exporters call `shelves()`, but use different margins and gaps, so a rendered SVG coordinate must never be reused for PDF or the theme preview.
- The plain piece model carries cut and seam polylines, unlabeled internal lines, notches, drills, grain and fold. It does not preserve the source path names or named seam segments. The chore-coat pilot resolves its design-local anchors before the piece model loses those names, then passes the resulting coordinates to both full-size exporters.

## 1. Define the shared data

- Give every visible cut piece a stable number in the design spec. Record its name, cut quantity, material, fold, and option conditions. Check numbers against the titles printed by the draft.
- Define named seam segments on the drafted pieces. Each seam code identifies the joining edges on both pieces, its notches, stitch type, and any special finish. Mark placement lines separately from joining seams, as the chore coat does with P6 and P7.
- Record pocket mouths and flaps, button and buttonhole positions, and other closure marks as drafted geometry with stable IDs. The skirt coat and pantry jacket need these records for future 3D renders as well as page instructions.
- Keep this metadata with the design rather than in page CSS or pixel coordinates. Validate unique piece numbers and seam codes, and reject references to missing or option-disabled pieces.

## 2. Generate the map from the draft

- Extend the preview/export pipeline to place labels on the actual seam paths and placement marks after the pieces are laid out. Resolve collisions with cut lines, notches, grain arrows, and the existing title block.
- Put the piece, seam, stitch, and finish codes directly on the page preview image and exported SVG/PDF, with a compact legend inside each image or document. Carry seam joins, pocket placements, buttonholes, and stitch/finish codes into the 3D render input. The design page's construction text explains the codes, so it does not need a separate stitch legend. Keep the diagram and its embedded legend readable when scaled to a phone or printed.
- Rebuild maps for the default and each published variant. Do not reuse coordinates from another size or option set. Show only active pieces and marks.

## 3. Connect construction text

- Store construction steps in structured data, with explicit piece-number, seam-code, and stitch-type references, while keeping explanatory prose in each design's brief. Generate the page steps and key from that data.
- Review the sewing order against the source and the drafted pieces. Identify proposed methods when the source does not specify assembly. Include preparation, joins, closures, and finishing in the order they are performed.
- Handle optional parts with conditional steps: a pocket, lining, sleeve, or closure appears only when its option is enabled. Keep the default page clear and provide variant-specific changes near each variant.
- Add a validation check that every referenced piece, seam, and stitch code exists and that each required joining seam is accounted for. Review stitch choice and finish for the fabric and garment; geometric checks alone cannot establish a workable assembly sequence.

## 4. Roll out by garment family

1. Refine the chore coat after review. Use it to establish the data schema, label placement rules, and page component.
2. Apply the schema to related work coats and jackets that reuse its body, sleeve, collar, and facing. Reuse shared seam definitions where their geometry is the same.
3. Continue with skirts and trousers, then dresses, shirts, outerwear, and other constructions. Audit each design's source brief before writing steps; do not infer a missing closure or finish as a source fact.
4. For every garment, inspect a default adult size, a child or stout size where supported, and option variants that change pieces. Compare the map with the exported cut pieces and check it at desktop and phone widths.

## 5. Page cleanup and completion criteria

- Keep the pattern map readable at full page width; let users enlarge it without losing the legend embedded in the image. Separate the finished-garment illustration, cut-piece map, construction, pattern editor, variants, and source notes with consistent headings and spacing.
- Test navigation anchors, keyboard access, screen-reader text for each code, light and dark themes, and print layout. Remove unrelated editor prompts from the map area only if they obscure the page in normal use.
- A design is complete when its preview, cut exports, and 3D render input agree on piece, seam, placement, closure, and stitch codes; every construction reference resolves; applicable variants regenerate correctly; the site builds; and a reviewer can follow the sequence on a test garment.

## 6. Review copyrighted source use before publication

Follow the project's guiding principle for any source still in copyright: reproduce the functional information needed to make the garment; create the digital pattern, explanations, and presentation independently; do not copy expressive material from the book. Use methods, formulas, and proportions as design references. Write construction steps from the finished draft, in our own words, rather than rephrasing the book's instructions. Keep source text, figures, scans, and page images out of the repository and published site.

After a design and its page are complete, **audit every in-copyright work consulted or processed before publishing**. Compare the draft, brief, construction steps, diagrams, labels, illustrations, and exports with those works. Check that each borrowed element is functional information and that the wording, artwork, layout, and other presentation are independently created. Record each work, what functional information was used, the audit result, and any changes made. Resolve any expressive copying or uncertain rights before the design is eligible for publication. Apply the repository's source-specific rules in `CONTRIBUTING.md` and `SOURCING.md` throughout this review.
