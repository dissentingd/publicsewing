## 1. Prepare the garment {#prepare}

- Photograph the whole garment: front, back and inside. Photograph details such as pockets, cuffs and closures before moving them.
- Write down where it came from, what the fabric seems to be, and anything you know about how it was made.
- Measure the finished garment laid flat: chest, waist, hem, length and sleeve where relevant. Record **cm or inches**, and whether a number is measured across the flat garment or all the way around it.
- Put a ruler in photographs of pieces so their size is clear. Keep a note of whether your tracings include <a href="/learn#seam-allowance">seam allowance</a>.

Next, follow the instructions for the garment you have:

- **[Rub-off](#rub-off):** the garment stays sewn together. Use this for something you wish to continue to be able to wear.
- **[Deconstruction](#deconstruction):** open its seams and trace each piece. Use this for a worn-out garment you are alright with destroying.

You can also submit measurements without traced pieces. Tell us what you measured; pieces and
clear dimensions give us more to work from.

The [Learn index](/learn#index) illustrates <a href="/learn#grainline">grainlines</a>,

## Rub-off: copy it without taking it apart {#rub-off}

You transfer one section at a time while the garment is still sewn. Have paper or inexpensive
muslin, pins, a ruler and a marking tool ready.

1. **Prepare the garment.** Press it gently and fasten its buttons or zip. Lay it on a flat surface that pins can go into, or over a layer of paper or muslin.
2. **Work on one section.** Start with a simple area such as half the back. Smooth it along the <a href="/learn#grainline">grain</a>, without stretching it. Pin along its seamlines.
3. **Transfer the shape.** Lay muslin over the section and rub over its seams with tailor's chalk. Or put paper underneath and push pins through the seams to leave marks on the paper.
4. **Record the details.** Mark the grainline, darts, pleats, pockets, closures and <a href="/learn#notch">matching notches</a>. Label the piece and say which side of the garment it came from.
5. **Check your tracing.** Join the marks into smooth lines. Measure each seam against the seam it joins; note any difference. Record whether you added seam allowance, and how wide.

<div className="test-callout"><strong>Layered detail?</strong> Pleats and hidden pieces may not transfer cleanly. Trace the base shape you can see, then measure and describe the part you cannot rub off.</div>

## Deconstruction: open the seams and trace {#deconstruction}

This method gives direct access to each piece, but the original garment will be taken apart. A
<a href="/learn#seam-ripper">seam ripper</a>, labels, paper, a ruler and a camera or phone are useful.

1. **Document it first.** Photograph the outside and inside from several angles. Write down the order in which the pieces appear to have been assembled.
2. **Mark before opening.** Draw the seamlines, grainlines and matching marks across joined seams. Label each piece. Mark where one edge is longer and eased into another, such as a <a href="/learn#sleeve-cap">sleeve cap</a>.
3. **Keep a reference if you can.** If both sides match, open only one side at first and leave the other assembled.
4. **Open the seams gently.** Use the seam ripper rather than cutting through the fabric. Start with main seams, then work through collars, cuffs and pockets.
5. **Trace the original pieces.** Press them lightly without stretching them. Trace the **seamlines**, not just the cut edges, and place a ruler beside each piece in a straight-down photo. Do not add seam allowance to the copy you send.
6. **Compare the copy.** If possible, sew a [practice garment](/sew-and-test) from your tracing and note where it differs from the original.

## Send your copy {#submit}

The [garment submission form](https://github.com/dissentingd/publicsewing/issues/new?template=garment-submission.yml)
is on GitHub. You need a free account to submit it. A GitHub issue is public, so leave out faces,
logos, printed artwork and labels from photos. Tell us how you would like to be credited.

<div className="test-checks">
  <div><h3>Pieces and measurements</h3><p>Send a PDF or DXF, or straight-down photos of flat pieces beside a ruler. List the pieces and say whether seam allowance is included. Add the garment's finished measurements with units.</p></div>
  <div><h3>Garment and story</h3><p>Add front, back and inside photos if you can. Describe the fabric, closures, pockets and construction order, plus the garment's maker or age as far as you know.</p></div>
</div>

The form asks for the garment, copying method, pieces, finished measurements, origin and credit.
It also asks you to confirm that you made the submitted pieces, notes and photos yourself and
license them under [CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/), which lets others
use them with credit. Read that permission before submitting. If the garment is a recent design
from a current maker, say so; we may decline it.

### What happens next

We check whether the pieces and measurements can support a new design. If they can, we add the
garment to our [sources](/sources) with your credit and put a design on the build queue for
[Inspired Designs](/inspired). The eventual pattern uses the garment as a reference and drafts
to a wearer's measurements; it will not be an exact copy. We cannot promise to build every
submission. If we cannot use yours, we will explain why on the issue.

## Learn more about copying garments {#learn-more}

- **One overview video:** [How To Copy ANY Piece of Clothing Into a Pattern](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wV2zQ07kCbo) (wynunlimited, 25 minutes).
- **Rub-off demonstrations:** UNT Fashion's [shirt preparation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qhH_hqPoE6g), [first technique](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ApLa2XDf2t8), [second technique](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DGpactpfUp0) and [trouser rub-off](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7Y54y5qxHVU).
- **Written guides:** Threads on [copying a beloved garment](https://www.threadsmagazine.com/2021/09/24/copy-a-beloved-garment) by Ruth Cabble, and [duplicating jeans](https://www.threadsmagazine.com/project-guides/sew-your-own-jeans/duplicate-your-favorite-jeans) by Raminta Vilkiene.
- **An easier first example:** Melly Sews' [T-shirt copy](https://mellysews.com/clone-your-t-shirt) uses the pin-and-paper method.
- **Books:** Tracy Doyle's *Patterns from Finished Clothes* and Steffani Lincecum's *Patternmaking for a Perfect Fit*.
